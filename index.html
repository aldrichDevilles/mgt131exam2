Module 3.1: Communication in Organizational Setting

What is Communication? It is simply the transference and understanding of meaning. If the other person didn't understand it, you didn't communicate; you just talked.

1. The Four Functions of Communication

  • Information: Giving people the facts they need to make decisions.
  • Control: Using rules, company policies, and job descriptions to control behavior (like a teacher setting classroom rules).
  • Motivation: Clarifying what to do and how to improve.
  • Emotional Expression: Venting frustrations or celebrating wins with your workgroup to fulfill social needs.

2. The Communication Model

Analogy: Texting your crush.
  • Sender: You.
  • Encoding: Figuring out how to turn your thought into words or emojis.
  • Message: The actual text you wrote.
  • Channel: Messenger or iMessage (the medium).
  • Decoding: Your crush reading it and trying to figure out what you meant.
  • Receiver: Your crush.
  • Noise: Anything that messes it up (bad internet, they were distracted, a typo).
  • Feedback Loop: Them replying so you know they understood.

3. Directions of Communication

  • Downward: Boss talking to employee (giving orders).
  • Upward: Employee talking to boss (giving progress reports or feedback).
  • Lateral: Coworker talking to coworker (same level).

4. Types of Interpersonal Communication

  • Oral (Speaking): Pros: Super fast, instant feedback. Cons: High risk of distortion if passed through many people (like the telephone game).
  • Written (Memos, Emails): Pros: Tangible, verifiable, well thought out. Cons: Takes time, no immediate feedback.
  • Non-verbal: Body movements, tone of voice, facial expressions, and physical distance.

5. Organizational Networks & The Grapevine

Formal Networks:

  • Chain: Strictly follows the chain of command (You -> Supervisor -> Manager).
  • Wheel: One central leader acts as the hub for all communication.
  • All-Channel: Everyone talks to everyone freely (like a group chat).

The Grapevine (Informal Rumor Mill):

  • It is NOT controlled by management.
  • Employees believe it more than official company emails.
  • It serves the self-interests of the people spreading it.
  • How to fix rumors: Don't shoot the messenger. Provide real information openly, explain decisions, and maintain open channels.

6. The 10 Barriers to Communication

  1. Delay: Information moves too slowly up the chain.
  2. Filtering: Manipulating info so the boss only hears the good stuff (hiding your bad grades).
  3. Short-circuiting: Skipping steps in the hierarchy (going straight to the principal instead of your teacher).
  4. Need for response: Expecting immediate feedback when it isn't possible.
  5. Selective Perception: Hearing only what you want to hear based on your own background/needs.
  6. Information Overload: Getting too many emails or instructions to process at once.
  7. Emotions: How you feel at the moment changes how you interpret a message.
  8. Language: Jargon, age gaps, or education levels changing the meaning of words.
  9. Communication Apprehension: Severe anxiety about speaking or writing.
  10. Lying: Straight up misrepresenting facts.

7. Current Issues in Communication

  • Gender Differences: Men use talk to emphasize status and are direct. Women use talk to create connection, apologize more to restore balance, and are less boastful.
  • Silence: It is not just "nothing." It communicates thinking, anxiety, agreement, dissent, or anger.
  • Politically Correct (PC) Communication: Using terms like "visually impaired" instead of "blind."
  • Cross-Cultural Barriers: Differences in semantics, connotations, tone, and conflict tolerance.
High-Context vs. Low-Context Cultures:
High-Context (Japan, China, Korea): Body language, status, and what is NOT said matters more than the words.
Low-Context (Europe, North America): Just say what you mean. Words matter most.

Module 3.2: Foundations of Group Behavior

1. Why do people join groups?

For Security, Status, Self-Esteem, Affiliation, Power, and Goal Achievement.

2. Formal vs Informal Groups

  • Formal Groups (Made by the company): Command groups (based on org chart, like a department) and Task groups (put together for a specific project).
  • Informal Groups (Made naturally): Interest groups (people playing in the company basketball league) and Friendship groups (bonding over common characteristics).

3. The 5-Stage Model of Group Development

  1. Forming: High uncertainty. Figuring out who is in charge and what the goal is.
  2. Storming: Conflict! Resisting constraints and fighting over who leads.
  3. Norming: Cohesiveness begins. The group agrees on rules and expected behavior.
  4. Performing: The group is fully functional and actually doing the work.
  5. Adjourning: Wrapping up the project and saying goodbye (for temporary groups).
Alternative Model for Temporary Groups (Punctuated Equilibrium):
Imagine a deadline for a group project. First meeting: you set a plan and do nothing for weeks (Phase 1 Inertia). Halfway to the deadline, you all panic (Midpoint Transition). You make a new plan and ride that out (Phase 2 Inertia). The night before it is due, everyone scrambles to finish (Final Burst).

4. Determinants of Group Performance

What makes a group succeed or fail?

  • External Conditions: The company's strategy, authority structure, formal rules, hiring process, reward systems, culture, available resources, and physical workspace.
  • Member Resources: The actual knowledge, skills, abilities, and personalities of the members.
  • Group Structure: (See section below for the deep dive).

5. Deep Dive into Group Structure

  • Formal Leadership: Crucial for success.
  • Roles: How you are supposed to act.
    • Role Identity: Your actual attitudes/behaviors for the role.
    • Role Perception: How YOU think you should act.
    • Role Expectations: How OTHERS think you should act.
    • Role Conflict: When your different roles clash (e.g., being a boss vs being a friend).
  • Norms: Acceptable standards of behavior. (Performance norms = how hard to work; Appearance norms = dress code; Social = who to eat with; Resource = who gets the best equipment).
  • Conformity: Peer pressure to change your behavior to match your Reference Group.
  • Deviant Workplace Behavior: Breaking the rules intentionally.
    • Production: Leaving early, working slowly.
    • Property: Sabotage, stealing.
    • Political: Favoritism, spreading rumors.
    • Personal Aggression: Sexual harassment, verbal abuse.
  • Status: Your social rank. Determined by your power, your critical contributions, or your personal traits (looks/intelligence). High status people can resist peer pressure better.
  • Size: Large groups (12+) are great for brainstorming and fact-finding. Small groups (7) are better for taking action. Watch out for Social Loafing (slacking off because you're in a group).
  • Composition: Diversity creates conflict initially, but leads to better, creative decisions later. Group demography / Cohorts: People who share a common attribute (like age or time at the company).
  • Cohesiveness: How much members like each other. High cohesiveness + High performance norms = Massive Productivity!

6. Group Processes

  • Synergy: 1 + 1 = 3. The group outcome is greater than individual parts. (Social loafing is negative synergy).
  • Social Facilitation Effect: Having people watch you makes you do simple, routine tasks faster, but makes you mess up on complex tasks that need deep focus.

7. Group Decision Making

Pros: More info, diversity of views, higher quality, more people accept the solution.

Cons: Takes forever, conformity pressures, one loud person can dominate, ambiguous responsibility (who gets blamed if it fails?).

  • Groupthink: Conforming to a bad idea just to avoid conflict with the group.
  • Groupshift: People take more extreme risks in a group than they would alone because the blame is shared.

Techniques:

  • Interacting: Regular face-to-face meeting. High social pressure.
  • Brainstorming: Throwing out all ideas with ZERO criticism.
  • Nominal Group: Everyone sits in a room but writes ideas silently and independently. (High number of ideas).
  • Electronic Meeting: Typing anonymously on computers. Fast and brutally honest.

Module 3.3: Basic Approaches to Leadership

1. Management vs. Leadership[cite: 53]

Analogy: The rule-enforcer vs the visionary.
  • Managers: Hold formal titles. They achieve results by directing activities and enforcing rules.
  • Leaders: Create a vision and inspire others to stretch beyond their normal capabilities. You don't need a formal title to be a leader.

2. Trait Theories (Are leaders born?)[cite: 54]

This theory focuses on personality. The 8 traits associated with leadership are: Drive, Desire to lead, Honesty and integrity, Self-confidence, Intelligence, Job-relevant knowledge, Extraversion, and Proneness to Guilt (because feeling guilty means you care about your responsibility to others).

3. Behavioral Theories (Can we teach you to be a leader?)[cite: 54, 55]

  • Ohio State Studies: Identified two behaviors: Initiating structure (focusing on the task, deadlines, rules) and Consideration (focusing on people, being friendly).
  • University of Michigan: Identified Employee-oriented vs Production-oriented leaders.
  • Scandinavian Studies: Added Development-oriented leaders (those who value experimentation and change).
  • Managerial Grid: A 9x9 graph measuring "Concern for People" vs "Concern for Production." The best is Team Management (9,9). The worst is Impoverished (1,1).

4. Contingency Theories (It depends on the situation!)[cite: 55, 56, 57, 58, 59]

  • Fiedler's Contingency Theory: Fiedler says your leadership style is FIXED. You take a test called the LPC (Least Preferred Co-worker). If you rate your worst coworker favorably, you are a "Relationship-oriented" leader. If you rate them poorly, you are "Task-oriented." Since you can't change your style, the company has to change the situation to fit you.
  • Hersey & Blanchard's Situational Leadership: Based purely on the "Readiness" (ability and willingness) of your followers.
    • Unable & Unwilling: Directing/Telling style.
    • Unable but Willing: Selling style.
    • Able but Unwilling: Participating style.
    • Able & Willing: Delegating style.
  • Path-Goal Theory: The leader's job is to clear the roadblocks for the followers. (Directive, Supportive, Participative, or Achievement-oriented).
  • Leader-Member Exchange (LMX): The "Favorites" theory. Leaders subconsciously create an In-Group (trusted employees who get privileges and work harder) and an Out-Group (employees who only get standard formal treatment and often quit).
  • Vroom & Yetton's Leader-Participation: A decision tree to decide how much employees should participate in making a choice. Ranges from Autocratic (leader decides alone) to Consultative (leader asks for ideas but decides) to Group-based (the group decides).

5. Contemporary Leadership Theories[cite: 59, 60]

  • Charismatic Leadership: Leaders with a vision who take high personal risks and behave unconventionally. The Dark Side: Narcissistic leaders who use their charisma for corrupt goals (like Hitler).
  • Transactional vs Transformational:
    • Transactional: Strictly business. Exchanges rewards for effort (Contingent Reward) and only intervenes when rules are broken (Management by Exception).
    • Transformational: Inspires you! Provides a mission, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration. The best leaders use BOTH.
  • Responsible Leadership:
    • Authentic: Highly self-aware, honest, and humble.
    • Ethical: Top leaders who set moral expectations for the whole company.
    • Servant: Focuses on helping the followers grow rather than personal glory.

Module 3.4: Power & Politics

1. What is Power?[cite: 48]

Power is the capacity that A has to influence B so that B acts according to A's wishes. It is entirely based on Dependency. The more B depends on A, the more power A has.

Leadership requires goal compatibility (we both want to win the game). Power does not (I'm holding your paycheck hostage, so you have to work).

2. The Bases of Power[cite: 49]

Formal Power (Based on your job title):

  • Coercive Power: Based on fear. The ability to punish or fire someone.
  • Reward Power: The ability to give out bonuses, good shifts, or promotions.
  • Legitimate Power: The formal authority of your job title (e.g., the Principal).
  • Information Power: Having access to data that others need.

Personal Power (Based on who you are):

  • Expert Power: Having special skills or knowledge (e.g., the IT guy fixing the server).
  • Referent Power: Based on charisma and being highly liked or admired (e.g., the popular kid).

3. What Creates Dependency?[cite: 49]

People depend on you if the resource you control is Important, Scarce (low supply of your skills), and Non-substitutable (no one else can do what you do).

4. The 9 Power Tactics[cite: 49, 50]

How people translate power into action:

  1. Legitimacy: "The employee handbook says you have to."
  2. Rational persuasion: Using data and logic.
  3. Inspirational appeals: Appealing to their values and hopes.
  4. Consultation: Asking them for input so they feel involved.
  5. Exchange: "If you do this for me, I'll do that for you."
  6. Personal appeals: Asking based on friendship.
  7. Ingratiation: Kissing up! Using flattery and praise before asking for a favor.
  8. Pressure: Threats and warnings.
  9. Coalitions: Teaming up with others to gang up on the target.

5. Sexual Harassment[cite: 50]

This is fundamentally about an abuse of unequal power in the workplace. Even coworkers (who don't have formal power) can harass peers by using their influence to withhold information or cooperation.

6. Politics: Power in Action[cite: 50, 51]

Political behavior means trying to influence the distribution of advantages in the company. Legitimate politics is normal (complaining to a boss, forming coalitions). Illegitimate politics breaks the rules (sabotage, whistleblowing).

The Labeling Game: Whether behavior is "good management" or "dirty politics" depends on your perspective.
- If you like them, they are "Delegating authority." If you hate them, they are "Passing the buck."
- If you like them, they are "Astute." If you hate them, they are "Opportunistic."

7. Impression Management (IM)[cite: 51]

IM is how you try to control the impression others form of you. Techniques include:

  • Conformity: Agreeing with someone to gain approval.
  • Excuses & Apologies: Trying to get pardoned for a mistake.
  • Self-promotion & Enhancement: Highlighting your best qualities or claiming your work is more valuable than it is.
  • Flattery & Favors: Kissing up to look perceptive and likeable.
  • Exemplification: Coming to work early and leaving late just to look dedicated.

8. Ethics of Political Behavior[cite: 51, 52]

How do you know if playing politics is ethical? Ask 3 questions:

  1. Is it motivated purely by self-interest and goes against the company's goals? (If yes, unethical).
  2. Does it respect the rights of the individuals affected? (If no, unethical).
  3. Is the activity fair and equitable? (If no, unethical).

Module 3.5: Conflict & Negotiation

1. Is Conflict Always Bad?

  • Traditional View: Yes, all conflict is bad and should be avoided.
  • Human Relations View: It is a natural occurrence, just accept it.
  • Interactionist View: A little bit of conflict is actually necessary! A completely peaceful group becomes lazy, apathetic, and static. You need some friction to spark innovation.

2. Types of Conflict

Analogy: The Group Project
  • Task Conflict (Good): Arguing over what topic to choose for the presentation. Low-to-moderate levels of this are highly functional.
  • Process Conflict (Good): Arguing over who should do the research vs. who makes the PowerPoint.
  • Relationship Conflict (Bad): Arguing because you genuinely hate your groupmate's personality. This is almost always dysfunctional.

3. The 5 Conflict-Handling Intentions

Imagine a graph where the Y-axis is Assertiveness (caring about yourself) and the X-axis is Cooperativeness (caring about the other person).

  • Competing (Win/Lose): High assertive, low cooperative. "I'm right, we are doing it my way."
  • Accommodating (Lose/Win): Low assertive, high cooperative. "Fine, whatever you want is okay."
  • Avoiding (Lose/Lose): Low assertive, low cooperative. Ignoring the problem entirely.
  • Collaborating (Win/Win): High assertive, high cooperative. Finding a creative solution where everyone gets exactly what they want.
  • Compromising (Tie): Meeting in the middle. Everyone gives up a little bit of what they want.

4. Negotiation (Bargaining) Strategies

  • Distributive Bargaining: Win-Lose. Imagine there is only one pizza (a fixed pie). If I take three slices, you only get one. We are fighting over limited resources (like labor vs management fighting over wages).
  • Integrative Bargaining: Win-Win. We work together to "expand the pie." Instead of fighting over the one pizza, we order a second one so both parties are completely satisfied.

Pro-Tip for Negotiating: Always know your BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement). This is your backup plan. It determines the absolute lowest offer you are willing to accept before you just walk away.

5. Third-Party Roles in Negotiation

  • Mediator: A neutral person who uses logic and persuasion to help you talk, but cannot force a decision (like a marriage counselor).
  • Arbitrator: Has the formal authority to dictate the agreement (like a judge in a courtroom).
  • Conciliator: A trusted informal communication link between two angry parties who refuse to speak face-to-face.

Module 3.6: Understanding Work Teams

1. Group vs. Team

A study group vs a basketball team.
  • Work Group: People who just interact to share information. They are evaluated individually. (Like sitting at the same table to do your own homework). Their synergy is neutral.
  • Work Team: People whose individual efforts result in positive synergy. They are mutually accountable for a collective goal. (Like a basketball team where 5 players working together can beat 5 individual superstars).

2. Types of Teams

  • Problem-Solving: People from the same department sharing ideas on how to improve things.
  • Self-Managed: Teams that do their daily work and take on the responsibilities of their former supervisors (no boss needed).
  • Cross-Functional: Employees from the same hierarchical level, but different departments (e.g., pulling someone from HR, IT, and Finance to build a new app).
  • Virtual: Using tech to tie physically dispersed members together.

3. Components of an Effective Team

How do you build a team that actually wins?

  • Work Design:
    • Autonomy: Giving the team freedom to schedule their own work.
    • Skill Variety: Letting workers use different talents so they don't get bored.
    • Task Identity: Letting the team complete a whole, identifiable piece of work (building the whole car, not just tightening one bolt all day).
    • Task Significance: Making sure the team knows their work actually impacts other people's lives.
  • Composition (Who is on the team?):
    • Size: The sweet spot is 5 to 9 members. If it gets bigger, cohesiveness drops and Social Loafing increases.
    • Diversity: Great for getting different viewpoints, though it can cause friction at the beginning.
    • Roles: Put your most capable, conscientious workers in the central roles.
  • Context: They need adequate resources from the company, a climate of trust, and a reward system that praises the whole team, not just the MVP.
  • Process: They need a clear common purpose, specific goals, and Team Efficacy (the genuine confidence and belief that they can succeed).
